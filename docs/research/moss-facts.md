# Moss facts check (slice 1: Surtsey pioneer to Mosses)

Fact-check of 11 claims. Source status: [F] = page read or search snippet this session; [M] = from memory, not verified, check before shipping a link.

## 1. Desiccation tolerance and revival speed
- Verdict: **supported**, but "minutes to hours" is the right order only for photosynthesis/membrane recovery. Full repair can take hours to a day. Not all mosses tolerate it (many aquatic or shade species do not).
- Wording: "Many mosses can dry out completely and resume photosynthesis within minutes of rewetting; full repair may take hours."
- Evidence: Syntrichia caninervis PSII yield reached 90%+ of its 30-min value within the first minute of remoistening [F]. Shoots showed damage at 0 h and recovered by 24 h in fast-dried treatments [F]. Dry storage over about 4 months recovered CO2 uptake only in short-stored samples [F].
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2802982 [F, snippet]; https://abs.bibl.u-szeged.hu/index.php/abs/article/view/2311
- Codex: "Many mosses can dry out completely, then restart photosynthesis within minutes of rain. Full repair can take hours."

## 2. Hair-tipped leaves, Racomitrium lanuginosum
- Verdict: **soften**. The hair points are real and make the moss look hoary when dry [F]. R. lanuginosum forms large mats on exposed rock. Its dominance on Icelandic lava is well known [F, via Surtsey paper below]. The function (less water loss, light reflection) is plausible and widely stated, but I found no primary source in this session. Say "may help", not "does".
- Wording: "Long, clear hair points give the moss a hoary look and are thought to reduce water loss and sun stress."
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racomitrium_lanuginosum [F]; https://britishbryologicalsociety.org.uk/learning/species-finder/racomitrium-lanuginosum [F]; Surtsey bryophytes: https://surtsey.is/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Surtsey-2022_15_6_Bryophytes-of-Surtsey-Iceland_61-87.pdf [F]
- Codex: "Woolly fringe-moss wears clear hair points on its leaves, thought to cut water loss and glare. It carpets Icelandic lava."

## 3. Peristome hygroscopic movement
- Verdict: **supported with a caveat**. Hygroscopic movement is restricted to arthrodontous peristomes (teeth made from cell-wall parts). Nematodontous peristomes (whole dead cells, e.g. Polytrichum) move less, but still help release. Dry-air opening and wet-air closing is standard. "Dry, windy" is a reasonable simplification; the sure part is humidity-driven. Sphagnum and Andreaea have no peristome.
- Wording: "In many mosses, peristome teeth bend with humidity, opening in dry air to release spores gradually."
- Sources: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11282784/ [F]; https://bmcplantbiol.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12870-024-05407-8.pdf [F]; https://blogs.ubc.ca/biology321/?p=68 [F]
- Codex: "Teeth around the capsule mouth bend with humidity. Many mosses open them in dry air to release spores."

## 4. Spores travel far on the wind
- Verdict: **supported**. Wind dispersal across the Southern Hemisphere is documented by floristic similarity and genetics. Surtsey got its mosses by long-distance dispersal.
- Wording: "Tiny moss spores can ride the wind for very long distances, including across oceans."
- Sources: Munoz et al. 2004, Science 304:1144, "Wind as a long-distance dispersal vehicle in the Southern Hemisphere" [M, DOI not checked]; Surtsey colonisation: https://english.surtsey.is/?p=1077 [F]
- Codex: "Moss spores are tiny and light. Wind can carry them across oceans, which is how islands like Surtsey get mosses."

## 5. Mosses trap dust and build soil
- Verdict: **supported (soften "slowly" is already right)**. Mosses and lichens are classic early successional soil formers. I did not fetch a specific primary source; the Surtsey papers describe moss/lichen cover preceding vascular plants on lava.
- Wording: "Moss mats trap dust and hold moisture, and their dead layers slowly become the first soil."
- Sources: https://english.surtsey.is/?p=1077 [F, context only]; look for a Surtsey soil/succession paper before using as a direct citation.
- Codex: "Moss mats catch dust and hold water. Layer by layer, their remains become the first thin soil on bare rock."

## 6. Surtsey: mosses and lichens
- Verdict: **supported with date caveats**.
  - First mosses Funaria hygrometrica and Bryum argenteum found in 1967 near steam vents; six more species in 1968 [F].
  - Mosses came **before** lichens. First lichen Trapelia coarctata in 1970, near the western crater (none in 1965, 1967, 1968) [F].
  - Colonisation of the open lava began around 1970: Racomitrium lanuginosum, plus lichens Stereocaulon vesuvianum and Placopsis gelida in 1970; R. ericoides in 1969 [F, snippet; not read in full].
  - Survey counts: 36 bryophyte species in 1971, 72 in 1972 [F, snippet].
- Do not say "Racomitrium was the first moss". Funaria/Bryum came first, in warm steam-damp spots, not on dry lava.
- Sources: https://english.surtsey.is/?p=1077 [F]; https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/11/4415/2014/ [F]; https://surtsey.is/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Surtsey-2022_15_6_Bryophytes-of-Surtsey-Iceland_61-87.pdf
- Codex: "On Surtsey, mosses came first: Funaria and Bryum appeared near steam vents in 1967. Lichens followed in 1970."

## 7. Sea rocket, first vascular plant
- Verdict: **supported, with a taxonomy note**. Found on the beach in spring 1965 (3 June 1965 in Fridriksson's report). Surtsey sources and UNESCO say Cakile arctica. The original 1965 report called it C. edentula; a dataset paper uses C. maritima subsp. islandica [F, snippet]. Safe wording: "sea rocket (Cakile)". The seedlings were buried by ash soon after, so establishment came later.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cakile_arctica [F]; https://english.surtsey.is/adflutningur-lifvera/ [F]; https://whc.unesco.org/document/152217 [F]
- Codex: "Surtsey's first vascular plant was sea rocket (Cakile), found on the beach in 1965, two years before the first mosses."

## 8. Tree form evolved several times
- Verdict: **supported**. Trees arose independently in lycophytes (Lepidodendron), sphenophytes (Calamites), ferns (tree ferns) and seed plants (including progymnosperm Archaeopteris). Caveats: Calamites was a large tree-like horsetail relative; the growth mechanisms differ (lycophyte and seed-plant wood are not homologous). "Giant clubmoss" is a fair popular term but they were not true clubmosses (Lycopodium).
- Wording: "Tree-like growth arose several times: in lycophytes, horsetail relatives, ferns and seed plants."
- Sources [M, pages not fetched]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepidodendron ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calamites ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_fern
- Codex: "Trees evolved more than once: scale trees (lycophytes), giant horsetail relatives, tree ferns and seed plants each grew tall on their own."

## 9. Land plant tree: bryophyte monophyly
- Verdict: **soften**. Bryophyte monophyly is not settled but is now well supported by recent nuclear and chloroplast data (Puttick et al. 2018; Sousa et al. 2019; Nishiyama 2004) [F, snippets]. Current common view: hornworts sister to Setaphyta (liverworts + mosses) [F]. An older view had liverworts as the first branch; hornworts-first and other arrangements still appear in 1KP analyses, so avoid "proven".
- Game split: "Land plants" splitting into "Mosses" and "Vascular plants" is fair if "Mosses" stays one of several bryophyte groups (liverworts and hornworts exist off-screen). It is also correct in either topology that mosses and vascular plants are separate lineages. Avoid drawing "Bryophytes" as the stem lineage ancestor of vascular plants.
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7373204 [F, snippet]; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6872490/ (1KP, Leebens-Mack 2019) [F, not read]; https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/108546/ (Puttick 2018) [F, snippet]
- Codex: "Mosses, liverworts and hornworts are all early land-plant lineages, each separate from the vascular plants that grew tall."

## 10. Key innovation for Mosses and precursors
- Peristome verdict: **poor as the key innovation for "Mosses" overall**. It defines the large group of peristomate mosses (Bryopsida), and nematodontous peristomes appear paraphyletic at the base of arthrodontous mosses [F]. Sphagnum, Takakia and Andreaea have no true peristome [F for Takakia; Sphagnum/Andreaea from memory]. Use it as a second-tier innovation for a subclade ("True mosses").
- Cox et al. 2004: stomata were absent in the ancestor of all mosses, and Sphagnum stomata are not homologous with others [F, snippet]. Do not use sporophyte stomata as a moss precursor.
- Moss-specific features from memory (verify before use): a filamentous protonema from which leafy shoots bud (gametophores); multicellular rhizoids with oblique cross-walls; a sporophyte with a seta and a capsule with a columella.
- My pick: **Key innovation = leafy gametophore budding from a protonema** (a "leafy shoot"), the most recognisable moss trait. Plan: precursors (in game order):
  1. Rhizoids: anchoring hair-like roots. Shared by all land plants (unicellular in liverworts and hornworts; multicellular in mosses and vascular plants).
  2. Desiccation tolerance (also shared widely among bryophytes, and a hallmark of early land plants).
  3. Sporophyte with seta and capsule (columella). Shared with liverworts to a degree (hornworts differ); a clear moss trait of the lineage.
  Alternative precursor if you prefer a flat one: "Leafy gametophyte", but leafy liverworts have it too (convergent).
- Sources: https://sapientia.ualg.pt/entities/publication/2e97831d-dfd1-4e80-ba34-c1d20a34913a/full (Cox et al. 2004, Syst. Bot.) [F, snippet]; https://wordpress.susqu.edu/course-diversityoflife/?p=8855 [F, snippet]
- Codex: "Mosses grow leafy shoots from a thread-like protonema. Most also gain peristome teeth, which meter out spores."

## 11. Growth forms (cushion, turf, mat)
- Verdict: **supported**. Mägdefrau's life-form system (1969, 1982) classifies mosses by colony shape: turfs, cushions, mats, wefts, etc. Colony shape strongly affects evaporation: one field study found life form lowers evaporation by a factor of about 5.3 to 46 [F]. Cushions and turfs hold water by external capillarity and suit exposed sites [F]. Mats/wefts spread horizontally. The "denser tufts" = water, "creeping shoots" = spread idea is a fair simplification. Caveat: many mosses switch form with conditions [F].
- Wording: "Dense cushions and turfs hold water; creeping mats spread across the ground."
- Sources: https://kirj.ee/wp-content/plugins/kirj/pub/ecol-4-1991-173-178_20230215162242.pdf [F]; https://revistas.uma.es/index.php/abm/en/article/view/22822 [F]
- Codex: "Dense cushions and turfs trap water between shoots. Creeping mats trade that for faster spread over bare ground."
