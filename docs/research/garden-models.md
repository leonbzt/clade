## Summary
1. Competitive exclusion is the default: without a stabiliser the best score takes the row. Replicator dynamics are mathematically the same as Lotka-Volterra (LV), so "use replicator growth" (P38 a) does not by itself stop a winner. The stabiliser does.
2. The cheapest stabiliser that fits an idle game is negative frequency dependence: a species' growth falls as its own share rises (Chesson's "stabilizing" mechanisms; kill-the-winner; Janzen-Connell; LV with crowding).
3. Equalizing mechanisms (trade-offs, a rubber band on the leader) only shrink the fitness gap. Alone they delay exclusion. Use both kinds.
4. Kill-the-winner models keep dozens of species alive with one rule: the bigger a species, the more it loses to its own enemy. Diversity rises with frequent, hard hits on the leader.
5. Top-N slots (the toy's `slots: 3`) are a hard stabiliser with a cliff at rank N+1. A soft self-limit gives the same "few winners" with no cliff, and has a closed-form equilibrium (see idea 1).
6. Predator-prey is the fragile part: type II predators plus rich prey cycle and crash (paradox of enrichment). Cheap fixes: refuges, predator self-limit, ratio-dependent kill.
7. Trophic cascades are real but weaker with omnivory and many species. Do not promise a clean chain. A few visible links are enough.
8. Catch-up: relaxation toward a target is stable at any step size if rate*dt is at most 1. With a self-limit term the long-run answer is computable directly. Only the swings need real stepping.
9. Game prior art is thin. I found no dev write-up that solves "one winner" for a many-species idle game. Thrive's auto-evo showed exclusion emerging with two species and coexistence with three.
10. Contradictions with `big-picture.md` and P38 are in the last section of this file.

## Ecology: what keeps many species alive
- **Chesson's frame (2000), as presented by Adler et al. 2007.** Coexistence needs stabilizing mechanisms (each species limits itself more than others; signature is negative frequency dependence) that outweigh fitness differences. Equalizing mechanisms shrink fitness differences but do not stabilise. Neutral = equal fitness, no stabilisation. Small fitness gaps need only weak stabilisation. [src: https://faculty.washington.edu/jhrl/Adleretal07.pdf ; original: https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/mechanisms-of-maintenance-of-species-diversity/ [search]]
- **Competitive exclusion (Gause).** Complete competitors cannot coexist at constant conditions; the holds only when factors are constant. Known ways out: spatial heterogeneity, trophic interactions, several resources, competition-colonization. Many models that allow coexistence allow unlimited species, while real communities are small. [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competitive_exclusion_principle]
- **Replicator = Lotka-Volterra.** The continuous replicator equation on n types is equivalent to generalized LV in n-1 dimensions. Share ratios change only through fitness differences; with fixed fitness that is exponential takeover of the log-ratio [calc/mem]. Frequency-dependent fitness is what makes interior (coexisting) equilibria possible; extinct types never return in the model. Discrete-time forms can period-double into chaos for strong games, so step size matters. [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replicator_equation]
- **Kill-the-winner (KtW).** Viruses hit whichever bacterial host is most abundant, so winners cannot dominate. Original: Thingstad & Lignell 1997, Thingstad 2000; it is framed as a solution to Hutchinson's paradox of the plankton. [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kill_the_Winner_hypothesis]
- **A KtW toy model you could copy.** Maslov & Sneppen 2017: N species share one resource (total pinned at capacity). Each grows at its own rate. At random times one species, picked with probability equal to its share, is multiplied by gamma much less than 1. Result: faster growers collapse more often, so slower ones are not excluded. More frequent and harder collapses give more diversity (it depends mainly on sigma*dt, the spread of growth rates times the mean interval). Rare collapses give plain exclusion. The slowest species still go extinct. The collapses are "cryptic": the total stays near capacity. [src: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5209715]
- **Janzen-Connell.** Host-specific enemies hit offspring near a parent or where the species is dense, so a common species leaves few safe sites. Evidence is real for some species and seedlings but mixed as a community-wide cause; few enemies are truly host-specific. Good game rule, weak "law". [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janzen%E2%80%93Connell_hypothesis]
- **LV with crowding.** Adding a mortality term that grows with own density (power delta) allows stable coexistence of 5 and 10 species in simulation, only when crowding is weak (delta near 1); strong crowding (delta up to 5) brings exclusion back. Simulations of one author; not field-tested. [src: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-19044-9]
- **Unlimited niche packing.** LV competition games with a trait axis can hold unlimited species until a minimum competition level or a limited trait range caps it. Useful as "why a finite list": limited trait range. [src: https://zenodo.org/records/3302993]
- **Oscillation-based coexistence.** Armstrong & McGehee 1980: one consumer with a nonlinear response makes cycles on which a linear one persists. Huisman & Weissing 1999: consumers on few resources coexist in much larger numbers through oscillations and chaos (6 on 3 resources; 12 on 5 in their examples). Swinginess can be the stabiliser, but it needs several resources and is hard to read. [src: https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/rescomp/vignettes/classic-results.html]
- **Random LV communities (Bunin 2017).** With random interactions there is a transition from one stable fixed point to history-dependent outcomes (multiple attractors) as interaction spread grows; a symmetry links the model to replicator equations. Takeaway: wild interaction tables give order-dependent, unpredictable results; keep interaction strengths narrow if you want predictable. [src: https://phsites.technion.ac.il/bunin/wp-content/uploads/sites/37/2018/08/PhysRevE.95.042414_LV_communities.pdf]
- **Predator-prey stability.** The paradox of enrichment (Rosenzweig 1971): more prey food can destabilise predators; with a type II response a Hopf bifurcation appears as carrying capacity K passes a threshold, and predators can go extinct. Stabilisers listed: refuges or invulnerable prey, ratio-dependent response (Arditi-Ginzburg), spatial heterogeneity, density-dependent predator mortality, inedible prey. [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_enrichment] (Wikipedia's equations did not render; I could not check the exact model form.)
- **Trophic cascades.** Hairston-Smith-Slobodkin "green world": predators cut herbivores, so plants flourish. Sea otter-urchin-kelp is the classic. Yellowstone wolves is contested and path-dependent (hysteresis: the system did not go back). Cascades are weaker with omnivory, diet shifts, plant defences, higher diversity; terrestrial meta-analysis found weaker cascades than aquatic (a later study disputes this). Mesopredator release is the "remove the apex, the mid-predator booms" effect. [src: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trophic_cascade]

## My own check [calc]
Script (scratchpad, not in repo). Scores 100/80/60/40/25.
- score^3 shares, top 3 slots: 0.58 / 0.30 / 0.13; the 4th and 5th get 0. Without slots: 0.55 / 0.28 / 0.12 / 0.04 / 0.01.
- Self-limited replicator (growth = score/100 minus m*own share): the equilibrium is "water-filling", p_i = max(0, (f_i - L)/m), with L found by sorting. m = 0.6 gives 0.67 / 0.33 / 0 / 0 / 0; m = 1.2 gives 0.50 / 0.33 / 0.17 / 0 / 0. Stepping the dynamics with dt from 0.1 to 3 landed on the same answer (0.664 / 0.331 / 0.005); dt = 8 drifted (0.598 / 0.393 / 0.010). So the closed form works and large steps are fine up to a point.
- Caveat: this is my construction from the KtW/crowding idea, one static fitness vector, no stocks. It shows the shape, not that it fits Clade's roles.

## The two code models against the criteria
(`toys/garden-sim.ts` is the sea garden toy; `src/sim/garden.ts` is the earlier plant garden, 3 layer slots, 1 s step. big-picture §4 calls the latter "reusable"; it is not role-based.)

**toys/garden-sim.ts**
- Good: swingy (seasons, 15% noise, 3% shocks that kill 35%, predator kill, stock feedback), and every change carries a reason string (readable). Role totals are pinned to a capacity from stocks (mats, detritus, oxygen), so the total is stable. Relaxation (rate 0.35) is unconditionally stable and cheap per generation; 40 generations of catch-up (`awayCap`) cost nothing. `arms` (natives you beat adapt, capped at 1.15 S) is a true equalizing mechanism. Spill and "natives only return where they belong" give a source-sink effect.
- Bad: within a role the share is fixed by score^3 and `slots: 3`, with no feedback from a species' own size, so exclusion is decided by score alone and the 4th species is cut to zero (a cliff: 60.1 vs 59.9 changes target from share to 0). Score only changes through Life eggs and `arms`, so a high-scoring line stays on top forever; that is the "one winner" risk. The predator removes the same fraction from every prey (`loss = kill * e.pop / prey`), so it does not punish the leader; it is not kill-the-winner. Predator capacity follows prey with a one-generation lag, which can ring; I did not run the toy to see (not checked).

**src/sim/garden.ts**
- Good: stocks (water, light, nutrient) and shade feedback give real niches per layer; `crowd: 0.85` makes losers shrink only as the slot fills, so newcomers crowd rather than empty a patch; weights are cached; spread along borders; natives keep a seed bank (`dieBelow`), so no native extinction.
- Bad: no top-N cap and no own-size penalty, so every species with w > 0 keeps a share until it falls below 1 g, which hides slow exclusion and then looks like sudden death; the leader's share is score^3 forever, with nothing pushing back. Cost is per second per species per slot: 8 h away is 28,800 steps over every patch and slot (it relaxes at 1/300 per second, about 5 min, so most of that is wasted). Closed form is exact only if weights are constant, and they depend on cover through shade and nutrient. Few swings other than the half-year; not tested in this pass.

## Contradictions with big-picture.md and P38
- **P38 (a) "replicator growth, newcomers earn ground in S-curves":** replicator dynamics with fixed fitness are winner-take-all (equivalent to LV competition). The S-curve is fine for newcomers, but it needs a self-limit or enemy term to stop the end state being one species. Option (a) as worded does not answer the P38 question.
- **big-picture §10 "Capped habitats and roles help":** a cap limits the total, not the share inside a row. In both code models the row's leader keeps score^3 of the cap. Only the toy's `slots: 3` limits the count, and that is a cliff.
- **big-picture §4 table says `src/sim/garden.ts` does "slots per role" after a change:** today it holds one slot per plant layer and no hard species cap; the toy's `slots` and role caps live only in the toy.
- **big-picture §6 item 6 "swings and history":** the toy's swings come from noise, season and shocks, not from internal dynamics; ecology says internal swings (oscillation) need more than one resource or a type II predator, and those are the unstable bits.

## Ideas for Clade
- **idea:** Replace `slots: 3` with a self-limit per species (growth minus m * own share), so the row's equilibrium is the closed-form water-filling and offline catch-up can jump to it, then replay only the last few generations for swings.
- **idea:** Make the top predator or a "blight" hit species in proportion to share squared (kill-the-winner), so booming lines crash on the news ("your worms are down to 40%"), and let m or the hit rate be the tuning knob for how many species a row keeps.
- **idea:** Give predators a prey refuge fraction and cap predator growth at its own self-limit, to keep the predator-prey pair from crashing when stocks are rich.
